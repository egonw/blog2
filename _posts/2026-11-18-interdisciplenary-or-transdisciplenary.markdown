[Jente Houweling](https://orcid.org/0009-0005-3680-0645) introduced me some time ago to the term
[transisciplenary](https://www.uu.nl/en/education/educational-development-training/knowledge-dossiers/interdisciplinary-education-and-cel/multi-inter-and-transdisciplinarity-what-is-what).
Where interdisciplenary combines modules of knowledge from multiple fields, transisciplenary
create more than the sums by integrating those modules of knowledge.

Chem- and bioinformatics as research fields should be like that. For me, they always have been
like that. They should not just apply data handling, but maximize the synergy of the knowledge
of the integrated domains. But that means that when doing transdisciplenary research, you need
to make a deep dive into all involved domains. This means continuous learning. But, well, that's
basically the job description of a researcher.

As a side note, one observation I make about the new, young generation of university students, is that many
of them would love to be studying for the rest of their live. That feels different from the
academic setting I grew up in, where not learning is the ideal, but counting papers and grants.

The research projects I have been involved in in the past 15 years, this transdisciplenarity has
been a key component. However, shared learning is by design not normally part of that: work
packages encourage people to work in silos. Shared conference calls to communicate help with
the communication, but make the research interdisciplenary at best.

Enter co-creation.

Fortunately, co-creation nowadays is a thing. Coming myself from the [C.U.C.](https://www.msx.org/wiki/Category:C.U.C._Computer_Info)
days, the idea of creating things in communities, these approaches feel very natural. This week
and the past two weeks I have been involved on various such meetings. I think one particular
part of the participative learning is just the biology of it: in terms of a recent book
I read, *The Molecule of More*, by Lieberman and Long, it brings the "up" to the "down". It
changes things we do not have into things we have in our hands.

This is a major difference between a designathon (doi:[10.14573/altex.2407211](https://doi.org/10.14573/altex.2407211))
and a hackathon (doi:[10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007808](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007808)): the first stays in the envisioning,
in the planning. It stays things in the future. But designathons do not bring things to
the here and now. And hackathons do just like that. And Bring-Your-Own-Data (BYOD) meetings
come very close too (doi:[10.1162/dint_a_00236](https://doi.org/10.1162/dint_a_00236)).

[Marc Teunis](https://orcid.org/0000-0002-3496-6669) and I proposed to start having hackathons in the very multidisciplenary
field of safety assessment, and I am happy we have held eight project-wide hackathons.
It think it is not an overstatement to say it may have saved the project: half-way the
project, there was no Virtual Human Platform yet.

I am happy that our project's story is now written up and published: "Promoting co-creation
and flexibility in transdisciplinary research projects on alternatives - the agile approach
of the virtual human platform for safety assessment project"
(doi:[10.1016/j.namjnl.2026.100154](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.namjnl.2026.100154)).
